#group-theory #math

**When you combine exponentials of non-commuting operators (BCH) or expand a time-ordered evolution (Magnus), every term is a nested commutator, and the coefficients in front are the same numbers whatever the operators are — because the expansion can be computed once, treating the operators as abstract letters that obey no rules at all.** Those letters and the words written with them form the free algebra, graded by word length. **In practice:** this is why BCH and Magnus coefficients are universal tables, why a series terminates exactly when your operators' nested commutators eventually vanish, and why "does the algebra close" is a question about your specific operators, not about the expansion.

A small subject: combinatorial algebra rather than analysis, and not usually part of a physics education.

# Reference

**Formally:** an alphabet, the words you can write with it, and no relations imposed at all — the free algebra is the universal object every other algebra is a quotient of, and grading it by word length is what makes the BCH and Magnus recursions bookkeepable.

**Picture:** letters with no rules: every word is distinct, and any actual algebra is obtained by declaring some words equal.

**Alphabet** $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_d\}$ — only $d$ symbols, no properties assumed.

**Word** — a finite sequence of letters, e.g. $x_1x_2x_2x_3$. Its **length** $|w|$ is the number of letters. The empty word is the identity, written $1$.

**Free monoid** $X^*$ — all words, with concatenation as the product. Associative and unital, and **nothing else**: $x_1x_2 \neq x_2x_1$, no letter is invertible, no word simplifies.

**Free associative algebra** $K\langle X\rangle$ — formal $K$-linear combinations of words. Multiplication is concatenation extended bilinearly.

**What "free" means, precisely.** Any map from the alphabet into an algebra extends uniquely to the entire free algebra:
$$X \to A \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K\langle X\rangle \to A \quad \text{uniquely}$$

So **every algebra generated by $d$ elements is a quotient of $K\langle X\rangle$.** "Imposing relations" means quotienting by the ideal they generate, and the free algebra is where you compute before deciding which relations to impose.

| Free object | Quotient by | Gives |
|---|---|---|
| $K\langle X\rangle$ | $XY - YX - [X,Y]$ for $X,Y\in\mathfrak g$ | $U(\mathfrak g)$ ([[Universal Enveloping Algebra and PBW Basis]]) |
| $K\langle a, a^\dagger\rangle$ | $aa^\dagger - a^\dagger a - 1$ | the Weyl algebra $A_1$ ([[Weyl Algebra and Degree Filtration]]) |
| $\mathrm{Lie}(X)$ | the relations among your generators | any Lie algebra generated by $X$ |
| free group on $X$ | relators | a group presentation ([[(Map) Group Theory]]) |

## Grading by length

$$K\langle X\rangle = \bigoplus_{n\geq 0} K\langle X\rangle_n, \qquad K\langle X\rangle_n = \mathrm{span}\{\text{words of length } n\}, \qquad \dim K\langle X\rangle_n = d^{\,n}$$

Because $|uv| = |u| + |v|$, **the product adds the degree** — so this is a grading, which is stronger than a filtration. It is the cleanest example of the distinction on [[Graded and Filtered Lie Algebras]], and it has the usual consequence: words of length $> N$ form an **ideal**, so truncating at length $N$ is passing to a quotient and is exact at that order ([[Subalgebras, Ideals, and Quotients]]).

Contrast the Weyl algebra, where imposing $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ *destroys* the grading: the relation equates a length-2 combination with a length-0 element, so only a filtration survives. **Relations are what break gradings.**

## The free Lie algebra

$\mathrm{Lie}(X) \subset K\langle X\rangle$ is the Lie subalgebra generated by the letters under $[u,v] = uv - vu$. Its elements are linear combinations of **bracket-words**: $x_1$, $[x_1,x_2]$, $[x_1,[x_1,x_2]]$, and so on. It inherits the length grading.

Bracket-words are over-determined — antisymmetry and Jacobi relate them — so a basis must be chosen. The dimension of the length-$n$ part follows the Witt formula
$$\dim \mathrm{Lie}(X)_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{e \mid n} \mu(e)\, d^{\,n/e}$$
giving, for two letters, $2, 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, \dots$ — one independent bracket at length 2 ($[x,y]$), two at length 3 ($[x,[x,y]]$ and $[y,[x,y]]$), and growth thereafter.

**Lyndon and Hall bases** are the standard canonical choices. A **Lyndon word** is one strictly smaller than all its rotations; the Lyndon words of length $n$ index a basis of the length-$n$ part. For practical use you only need to know that such a canonical basis exists and is indexed combinatorially, not how to construct it.

## Why this is the right language for BCH and Magnus

**BCH is a theorem in the free Lie algebra.** $\log(e^Xe^Y)$ is an element of $\mathrm{Lie}(\{X,Y\})$, completed by degree, and each degree-$n$ term is a *universal* Lie polynomial with fixed rational coefficients — computed once, valid for every substitution. That universality is the theorem's content, and it is a statement about words, not about matrices ([[Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff]]).

**Magnus is the same, graded by slot count.** $\Omega_k$ is a fixed combination of length-$k$ bracket-words in $A(t_1),\dots,A(t_k)$, integrated over the time simplex. The recursion generating $\Omega_k$ is a recursion on words, and each word is a $k$-linear map awaiting arguments ([[Multilinear Maps and Polarization]], [[Magnus Expansion]]).

Three things this viewpoint makes obvious:

| Statement in your algebra | Statement about words |
|---|---|
| "the series terminates" | the map $\mathrm{Lie}(X)\to\mathfrak g$ sends all words beyond some length to zero — **ad-nilpotency** ([[Conjugation]]) |
| "the algebra closes at dimension $m$" | words beyond some length become linearly *dependent* on shorter ones; $\mathfrak g$ is the image of $\mathrm{Lie}(X)$ and the relations collapse the count ([[Dynamical Lie Algebras and Closure]]) |
| "truncating at order $N$ is exact" | the discarded words are the length-$>N$ ideal |

The middle row is the useful reframing: **"does my dynamical Lie algebra close?" is the question of which bracket-words become dependent** once you substitute actual Hamiltonians for the letters. Closure is not a property of the words — they never close — but of the substitution map.

> [!question]- Why is BCH a universal formula rather than a fact about particular operators?
> It is computed in the free Lie algebra on two letters, where no relations hold, so the result is forced to be a combination of abstract bracket-words with fixed coefficients. Substituting your actual operators afterwards is a homomorphism, so the identity transports — and any simplification (terms vanishing, series terminating) comes from *your* relations killing words, not from the formula.
