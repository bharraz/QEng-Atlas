#solid-state

**Cool identical bosons below a critical temperature and a macroscopic fraction crowds into the single lowest-energy state, forming one coherent matter wave.** The same condensation logic underlies superfluids, superconductors, and cold-atom BECs — it is the bridge between AMO and solid state.

# Reference

Bosons have no exclusion principle, so nothing stops many of them occupying one state. Below the critical temperature $T_c$ the ground-state occupation becomes macroscopic (of order $N$). The onset condition is that the thermal de Broglie wavelength reaches the interparticle spacing:

$$n\,\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}^3 \approx 2.612, \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{dB}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}}.$$

The condensate is described by a single **order parameter** $\psi = \sqrt{n}\,e^{i\varphi}$ — one wavefunction for the whole population, carrying a definite macroscopic phase. That shared phase is what produces coherence: two released condensates interfere like laser beams. Interactions are handled by the Gross–Pitaevskii equation.

**Where it appears:** dilute alkali gases at $\mu$K in magnetic/optical traps (reached by laser then evaporative cooling — see [[Doppler Cooling]]); liquid $^4$He as a superfluid; and paired electrons in a [[Superconductivity|superconductor]]. Fermions must first pair into bosons (Cooper pairs) before they can condense.

> [!question]- What distinguishes a BEC from simply "a cold gas where most atoms sit in the ground state"?
> Coherence and a single macroscopic phase. The condensate is one wavefunction, not a mere population statistic, so two independent condensates released and overlapped show interference fringes. It is the phase coherence — not just low energy or high ground-state occupation — that makes it a distinct state of matter.
